      Alignment settings
        
      
          
      
          
      
          
      
          
      
          
      
          
      
        
      
        
      GPS matches the two tracks by position and heading (resampled every 2 m) and trims both rides to the section they share; every point gets a timing uncertainty from the GPS drift, speed and gaps between fixes. The pots and IMU then fine-tune the timing in two passes (10 Hz inside that uncertainty, then the fine rate within ±0.35 s), with the warp speed limited to 0.5–2× so it can't jitter on noise. The search window caps how far the sensors may move the timing from GPS. GPS drift: about 3.5 m for a 1 Hz receiver; lower it for a 10 Hz or RTK unit.

        Time gap
        
          
        
          
        
          
        Sectors: where and why
        
          
        Tap a sector to zoom both charts to it.
        
          
        
          
        Line: on the same line the pots and IMU see the same bumps in the same order, so the two rides line up distinctively and pots-only and IMU-only alignments agree. On a different line they don't: the match is no better than a shifted one, the two sensor groups disagree, or the warp has to jerk to force a fit. GPS adds a lateral offset when B is several metres off A's line. Why: on the same line the terrain is identical, so a fork sitting deeper relative to the shock (more nose-down) points to braking or weight forward, and harder hits on the same bumps to more speed into them. Offsets that hold for the whole run (setup, sag) are taken out first. With GPS and a different line, the gain is split into line length and speed.

            How it stays robust: everything comes from one fixed 4 s version of the gap, whatever the Speed tab's smoothing. A mistake is measured as a step between the gap's level in the 2 s before and the 2 s after, against the pace around it (the median over 24 s, where the pots/IMU count more than GPS), so an alignment wobble that comes straight back isn't a loss. It is listed when it is at least 0.2 s, sharp (8% or more on top of that pace while it lasts) and 3.5 sd clear of the alignment noise (the Monte Carlo draws). The bar is higher than usual because the biggest of many windows is picked, and the biggest bit of noise among them is itself 2.5–3 sd. A slow stretch is the 10 s or more with the most time lost above the rider's own average, with the listed mistakes taken out so nothing is counted twice. It is listed when it is at least 0.3 s and 3 sd clear. At most 3 mistakes and 2 slow stretches per rider. Rough timing: the pots/IMU don't lock on there, so it comes from GPS or the rough alignment and is good to a few seconds. Causes are what stands out against the other rider there, not proof.

            Each hit is measured in both rides and B − A is taken per hit. q is the p-value corrected for testing many metrics at once (Benjamini–Hochberg); a metric counts as significant when q < 0.05. Phase counts and the IMU compression / rebound values use the split speed set in Setup (the Python tool used 10 mm/s, which counts sensor noise as movement). IMU acceleration is the size of the acceleration vector minus 1 g, so it doesn't depend on how the sensor is mounted. dz is the mean change divided by its spread (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large). Hits a few seconds apart share speed, line and fatigue, so p-values are optimistic: ride each setup more than once (A, B, A, B) before trusting a small change.

            Hit detection & statistics (Compare)
        
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
          
            
        
            
        
            The same rules find hits in both rides. A hit starts at the lowest point in the look-back window before velocity passes the threshold, peaks at the deepest point before rebound, and ends at the lowest point once it is back within the set share of its travel and calm, at the next hit, or at the maximum length. Hits are paired when their peaks land within the pairing window on the aligned timeline; a hit found in only one ride is measured in the matching window of the other, so neither ride decides which hits count.
            
      
            
      
            
        
        
      
            
    
            
    
            
      
            About the numbers
            
      
            Sample rate is read from each file's time column (logger files: 1000 Hz sampled, 4 averaged → 250 rows/s). Velocity uses a Savitzky-Golay derivative computed for the chosen window and order. Positive velocity is compression everywhere. Results differ from the old SD-card analyzer, whose velocities were about 12× too high (it assumed 1000 Hz and its filter weights summed to 3.14 instead of 1).
            
      
            Compare lines the rides up in two stages: GPS first (tracks matched by position and heading), then the pots and frame/bar IMU together (coarse-to-fine DTW with a limited warp speed). The pots-only and IMU-only alignments are also run separately as a cross-check. Speed difference is the slope of the time gap: the gap is fused from the sensor timing (where trusted) and GPS, smoothed, and differentiated; its band comes from 100 simulated re-runs with each input's own noise, widened so it holds for the whole run at once. Frame and bar IMU readings are converted to g (logger files are already in g; older files in m/s² are detected).
            
      
            Compare map: the trail (from A's GPS, or B's carried over through the alignment) is coloured by the speed curve on the Speed tab, as the faster rider's lead in % (blue A, orange B, grey even). The colour scale is fixed per comparison at the 95th percentile of that difference while both riders are moving and the sensors are trusted, rounded up to 3, 5, 8, 10, 15, 20, 25 or 30%; stops (where the alignment has one rider at least 4× slower for 3 s or more: one clock runs on while the other covers the same ground) are taken out of the comparison, drawn dark grey and left out of the scale, and anything beyond the scale is drawn at full colour and ringed. The two dots start the section together and move by the alignment's time gap (stops included), so a rider who stopped waits there. Everything in the comparison comes from the alignment; the GPS only places the trail on the map.
            
      
            Without GPS the rough alignment comes from the sensors themselves: front and rear travel and the frame and bar IMU are matched by shape (not size, so a different spring, shock or sag still matches) over the whole ride, then in 8 s windows, and the windows are chained into one timeline. Only stretches where both riders are moving are matched, so waiting at the start doesn't count. Independent check: every comparison re-measures the timing a second way, by plain cross-correlation in 4 s windows with no DTW, and says whether the bumps in the speed curve are real. On two real runs with different shocks (2000 Hz, no GPS) the result agreed with it to about 25–30 ms where trusted, and the speed to about ±1.5% over 5 s. Sample rate: settings counted in rows mean rows at 250 a second and are scaled for files at other rates, so they always cover the same time.
